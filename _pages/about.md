I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at The Ohio State University. My research interests are in **labor economics**, **health economics**, and **public policy**.

I study how labor market regulations and social insurance programs affect employment, earnings, and well-being. My research focuses on **labor market information and wage determination, labor supply, and health and social insurance programs**, with particular interests in **information frictions, labor market concentration and competition, workers' bargaining power, and public health insurance**.

## Job Market Paper

### The Effects of Colorado's Pay Transparency Law on Older Workers

This paper studies how cross-firm pay transparency affects labor market outcomes of mid- to late-career workers. Using Colorado's Equal Pay for Equal Work Act (EPEWA) and a difference-in-differences design, I find that pay transparency increases weekly earnings and hourly wages. The gains are larger for women, more-educated workers, and workers closer to retirement, and the law narrows the gender wage gap. Both job changers and stayers experience wage gains, although the gains are larger among changers. Wage effects also vary across occupations and are larger in local occupational labor markets with greater concentration. The policy also increases employment and working hours in the short run. By contrast, effects are limited among younger workers. Survey evidence on workers' beliefs further shows that, among older workers, pay transparency changes their expectation about outside options and reservation wages. Overall, the findings suggest that cross-firm wage information is particularly valuable for mid- to late-career workers, who may face relatively high search frictions and have outdated wage information.

## Publication

### Balancing Efficiency and Equity in Classroom Assignment under Endogenous Peer Effects

*with Lei Bill Wang, Zhenbang Jiao, and Om Prakash Bedant*

**Published in *Journal of Applied Economics***

This paper develops a three-step empirical framework to optimize classroom assignments under endogenous peer effects. First, we design PeerNN, a neural network modeling friendship formation, generating a linkage-intensity matrix (Ω) that captures student popularity. Second, using quasi-random classroom assignments as an instrument, we estimate peer effects: a 10% increase in friends’ 6th-grade class rank (weighted by Ω) increases 8th-grade test scores by 0.13 SD. Third, we simulate assignment policies. A genetic algorithm (GA) maximizing average peer effect yields a 1.9% gain but exacerbates inequality, disproportionately harming students with lower 6th-grade class ranks. We propose a variance-penalizing approach named Algorithmically Fair GA (AFGA), which achieves a 1.2% improvement while ensuring equitable outcomes. Our findings highlight the trade-off between efficiency and equity in classroom assignment policies and underscore the need for fairness-aware algorithms when designing group assignment policies.

**First place, 2024 Hayes Forum Poster Competition.**
